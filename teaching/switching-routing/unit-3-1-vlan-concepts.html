VLAN concepts, purpose, and membership

Unit 2 treated a switch as one flat network — every port, one broadcast domain. This unit changes that: VLANs let a single physical switch behave like several independent logical switches, and this section covers what problem that solves, the VLAN types and numbering we'll actually see, and how to create a VLAN and put a port in it.

Problem statement: a switched network with redundant links, one afternoon, grinding to a halt for no obvious reason. We'll work backward from that condition into broadcast domains, collision domains, why loops form at Layer 2 at all, and — once that's clear — exactly what problem VLANs and Spanning Tree are each separately solving.

Problem: a network that was fine an hour ago

Before any definitions — let's sit with this condition and predict what happens next.

Condition A small office has two access switches, SW-A and SW-B, connected for redundancy by two separate cables — Gi0/1 and Gi0/2 on each side — so that if one cable fails, the other keeps the office connected. No routing is involved; both switches are plain Layer 2 devices, both links are simply plugged in and left up. Around 2 p.m., with no new device added and no configuration change made by anyone, every computer in the office slows to a crawl, then stops responding entirely. Rebooting a PC doesn't help. Unplugging one of the two cables between the switches instantly fixes everything.

Two cables between the same pair of switches, both active, is an everyday redundancy design — engineers add a second link specifically so a single cable failure doesn't take the office offline. And yet here, having that second link is exactly what caused the outage. Before moving on, it's worth pausing on the question: why would adding a redundant path cause a failure, rather than prevent one? And why does unplugging a cable — removing capacity — make the network faster, not slower?

Answering this requires two ideas we usually first hear about in the context of VLANs, but which are really more fundamental than VLANs: broadcast domains, and what a switch does with a frame it doesn't know where to send.

Broadcast domain vs. collision domain

These two terms get used almost interchangeably early on, and they describe genuinely different problems, bounded by different devices.

Collision domainBroadcast domain
What's sharedThe physical medium — two devices transmitting at the same instant corrupt each other's signal.Broadcast (and unknown-unicast, and multicast) traffic — every device in it receives a copy of every broadcast frame sent by any member.
What creates a boundaryA switch port. Each switch port is its own collision domain — this is the reason switches replaced hubs.A router interface, or a Layer 3 boundary (an SVI). A switch, by itself, does not break up a broadcast domain — it floods broadcasts out every port.
On a modern switchEffectively eliminated. Full-duplex, dedicated switch ports mean two devices almost never contend for the same wire — collisions are largely a half-duplex/hub-era problem.Still very much alive. Every port on an unmanaged flat switch, and every switch trunked to it, is one single broadcast domain until something — a router, or a VLAN plus a router — divides it.
What crossing the boundary requiresNothing — collision domains aren't "crossed," they're just isolated per port automatically.Layer 3 forwarding. A frame doesn't leave a broadcast domain by switching; it leaves by being routed.
Why this matters for the scenario above Because collisions are essentially a non-issue on modern switched, full-duplex links, the office's outage in the scenario has nothing to do with collision domains at all. It's entirely a broadcast-domain problem — and specifically, a problem with what a switch does when it doesn't yet know which port leads to a destination.

Practice: counting collision and broadcast domains

Counting domains on a mixed topology is a classic exercise for testing whether the difference above is actually understood — it's the same style of question found in CCNA prep material such as Todd Lammle's CCNA Routing and Switching Complete Study Guide, one of this course's prescribed texts. The topology below is an original diagram built in that same spirit, not a reproduction of any specific textbook figure.

A hub (orange) repeats bits to every attached device with no awareness of frames at all; a switch (blue) forwards frame-by-frame per port; a router (green) is the only device that bounds a broadcast domain.

Question In the topology above, how many collision domains are there, and how many broadcast domains?

Collision domains: 9. Every hub collapses all of its attached devices, plus the link leaving it, into a single shared collision domain — so HUB1 with PC1, PC2, PC3, and its uplink to SW1 counts as just one (1). Every other point-to-point link — SW1↔PC4, SW1↔PC5, SW1↔R1, R1↔R2, R2↔SW2, SW2↔PC6, SW2↔PC7, and SW2↔HUB2 — is its own separate collision domain, since a switch port isolates whatever is on the other end of that specific cable. That's 1 (Hub1's domain) + 8 individual switch/router-port links = 9.

Broadcast domains: 3. Nothing in this topology is a router boundary except R1 and R2 themselves — switches and hubs never separate a broadcast domain, no matter how many are chained together. Everything on R1's LAN side (HUB1, PC1–3, SW1, PC4, PC5) is one broadcast domain; everything on R2's LAN side (SW2, PC6, PC7, HUB2, PC8, PC9) is a second; and the R1↔R2 serial link, bounded by a router interface on each end, is conventionally counted as its own third broadcast domain even though a point-to-point WAN link rarely carries broadcast traffic in practice.

How a loop actually forms, step by step

This is the mechanism behind the scenario above — and it's a direct, mechanical consequence of two switch behaviors that are each individually correct in isolation.

SW-Aaccess switch
⇆Gi0/1
SW-Baccess switch
SW-Asame switch
⇆Gi0/2
(second, redundant cable)
SW-Bsame switch

Two active links between the same two switches, with nothing blocking either one — a physical loop.

Something ordinary — an ARP request, a DHCP discover — arrives at SW-A from a connected PC. Nothing unusual yet; this happens constantly on any live network.

A broadcast frame, by definition, is supposed to be flooded out every port except the one it arrived on. SW-A does exactly this — including out both Gi0/1 and Gi0/2, since both lead toward SW-B. This is correct, expected switch behavior.

SW-B has no way to know these two frames are actually the same broadcast — Ethernet frames carry no loop-detection field, no hop count, no TTL. Each copy is treated as a new, legitimate broadcast and flooded out every port except the one it arrived on — which includes sending the copy that arrived on Gi0/1 back out Gi0/2 toward SW-A, and vice versa.

SW-A now receives those two returning copies back from SW-B, and — following the same correct flooding rule — sends each back out to SW-B again. With no mechanism to age out or recognize a frame as "already seen," the copies multiply on every pass, at wire speed, indefinitely. This is a broadcast storm.

The original host's source MAC address arrives at SW-B on Gi0/1 and, moments later, on Gi0/2 as well — the same address, from two directions. Each switch keeps re-learning that MAC against whichever port it most recently arrived on, overwriting the table entry back and forth. This is called MAC address flapping, and it means legitimate unicast traffic to that host gets misdirected on top of everything else.

No TTL is the root cause An IP packet has a TTL field specifically so a routing loop eventually dies on its own. An Ethernet frame has no equivalent. A Layer 2 loop, once formed, does not resolve itself, does not slow down, and does not expire — it multiplies until something external intervenes: a link is physically pulled, an interface is shut down, or a loop-prevention protocol blocks a port before the storm ever starts.

Isn't Spanning Tree supposed to prevent exactly this?

Yes — and this is worth being precise about, because it's commonly misremembered the wrong way around.

STP is enabled by default on Cisco switches — not disabled Out of the box, Cisco Catalyst switches run a form of Spanning Tree (PVST+, per-VLAN) automatically on every VLAN, with zero configuration required. In a real deployment of the two-switch scenario above, STP would detect the redundant path between SW-A and SW-B, elect one link as the active forwarding path, and put the other into a blocking state before a storm could ever start — the second cable stays physically connected, but STP simply never lets traffic use it while the primary path is healthy. The scenario at the top of this page describes what Layer 2 does without that protection — which in practice means STP has been explicitly turned off, a non-STP-aware device is in the path, or a feature like BPDU filtering has suppressed it on that link.

So the honest version of the question is: if STP were disabled, or missing, or blocked from working — what does that cost in switch resources? The scenario above already showed the network-level symptom (a storm); here's specifically why it's expensive for the switches themselves, not just for the wire:

!

CPU utilization climbs toward, and often hits, 100%

Flooding is not free — every single copy of every duplicated frame is a forwarding decision the switch's CPU or forwarding ASIC has to make. As the frame count doubles on every pass, the switch spends essentially all of its processing capacity flooding frames instead of doing anything else — including responding to the very console or SSH session an administrator would use to fix it.

!

Bandwidth on every trunk in the loop saturates almost instantly

Because the frame count grows geometrically rather than staying constant, a link that was carrying a trivial amount of broadcast traffic a second earlier can hit full line-rate utilization within seconds — leaving no capacity for real user traffic on that same link.

!

The MAC address table churns instead of stabilizing

Continuous MAC flapping (Step 5 above) means the switch is constantly rewriting table entries rather than settling into a stable, low-effort steady state — a further, ongoing CPU cost layered on top of the flooding itself.

!

The failure is self-reinforcing, which is what makes it "voracious"

Each of the three effects above feeds the others: more flooded frames mean more MAC relearning, which consumes more CPU, which slows the switch's ability to process anything — including, ironically, STP's own BPDU messages if the protocol were only partially working rather than fully absent. Unlike a gradual resource leak, a Layer 2 broadcast storm can take a switch from idle to unresponsive in well under a minute.

Why VLANs exist

Without VLANs, every port on a switch — and every switch trunked to it — sits in exactly one broadcast domain. Every ARP request, every DHCP discover, every broadcast from any device reaches every other device on that switch, whether it has any reason to or not.

This connects directly back to the storm above: VLANs make each broadcast domain smaller, which shrinks the blast radius if a loop or storm ever occurs — but VLANs alone do not stop a loop from forming. That's Spanning Tree's job, running independently inside each VLAN. The two mechanisms solve different problems and are both necessary.

VLAN 10 (Sales), VLAN 20 (HR), VLAN 30 (IT) — a broadcast in one VLAN never reaches the others, regardless of which physical port a device plugs into.

The core idea: a VLAN is a logical grouping of ports into separate broadcast domains, independent of physical location or which switch a device happens to be plugged into. That decoupling is where most of the practical benefit comes from:

Smaller broadcast domains

Fewer devices per broadcast domain means less broadcast traffic reaching each host, and less exposure if one segment misbehaves.

Logical security boundaries

Finance and guest Wi-Fi traffic can sit on the same physical switch but different VLANs, with a router or Layer 3 switch controlling exactly what's allowed to cross between them (Unit 3.3).

Grouping by function, not geography

A user can move desks, or even floors, and keep the same VLAN membership — the switch port they land on is configured to match, not the other way around.

Fewer physical switches needed

Before VLANs, separating departments meant separate physical switches and separate cabling. One switch with VLANs replaces what used to require several.

VLAN types and numbering ranges

Not every VLAN carries the same kind of traffic, and not every VLAN number is available for ordinary use.

VLAN typePurpose
Default VLAN (1)Every port belongs to VLAN 1 out of the box. It also carries control-plane traffic like CDP by default. It cannot be renamed or deleted — leaving ordinary user devices on it is a common and avoidable security weak point (see Tips).
Data VLANOrdinary user/device traffic — the VLANs created for Sales, HR, IT, and so on in the diagram above.
Voice VLANA separate VLAN carried alongside the data VLAN on the same access port, used for IP phones — lets phone traffic get separate QoS treatment from the PC plugged into the phone's pass-through switch port.
Management VLANThe VLAN carrying the switch's own management IP (its SVI). Best practice keeps this separate from VLAN 1 and from any general user VLAN, so managing the switch isn't exposed on the same broadcast domain as ordinary devices.
Native VLANThe one VLAN on a trunk link whose traffic is sent untagged. Covered in depth in Unit 3.2 (trunking) — introduced here only because it's still, technically, a VLAN type.
RangeVLAN IDsNotes
Normal range1–1005The range used in almost all campus designs. 1002–1005 are reserved for legacy Token Ring/FDDI VLANs and can't be used or deleted on most platforms.
Extended range1006–4094Available on modern switches for larger environments (service providers, very large campuses). Historically not carried by VTP versions 1/2 — VTP transparent mode or VTPv3 is needed to use this range with VTP in play.

Port modes: access (untagged), trunk (tagged), and hybrid

Every switch port runs in one of these modes, and the mode decides one thing above all else: whether a frame leaving that port carries a VLAN tag or not.

Tagged vs. untagged, in one sentence An untagged frame relies on the receiving port's own configured VLAN to decide where it belongs; a tagged frame carries a 4-byte 802.1Q header stamped with the VLAN ID directly in the frame, so a single physical link can carry traffic for several VLANs at once without ambiguity.
ModeTagging behaviorVLANs carriedTypical use
AccessAlways untagged. The port has one VLAN (its PVID) and never adds or expects an 802.1Q tag.Exactly one data VLAN (plus, optionally, one separate voice VLAN — see below).A port connected to an end device — a PC, printer, or AP in single-SSID mode — that has no VLAN awareness of its own.
TrunkTagged for every VLAN except the native VLAN, which is sent untagged by default.Multiple VLANs simultaneously, restricted by an allowed-VLAN list.A link between two switches, or between a switch and a router/firewall/AP that itself needs to see multiple VLANs.
HybridConfigurable per VLAN, per port — some VLANs tagged, others untagged, on the very same link at the same time.Multiple VLANs, with tagging decided VLAN-by-VLAN rather than all-or-nothing.Not a Cisco IOS mode. Common on Huawei/H3C and other vendors' switches, and used for the same scenarios an access port with a voice VLAN handles on Cisco gear — see the note below.
Cisco IOS doesn't actually have a mode called "hybrid" Cisco's switchport mode only accepts access or trunk (plus the legacy negotiating modes dynamic auto / dynamic desirable, covered in Unit 3.2). The concept a hybrid port describes — one link carrying both tagged and untagged VLANs at once — is exactly what an access port with a voice VLAN already does on Cisco: the PC's traffic on VLAN 10 leaves untagged, while the phone's traffic on VLAN 110 leaves tagged, on the same physical cable. Other vendors formalize this as its own explicit port type named "hybrid"; Cisco reaches the same result through the access-plus-voice-VLAN pattern instead. Worth knowing both terms exist so a Huawei/H3C config isn't misread as unfamiliar when it's describing something already covered.
Cisco IOS commandWhat it sets
switchport mode accessFixes the port as access mode — untagged, single VLAN.
switchport access vlan 10Sets which VLAN that access port's untagged traffic belongs to.
switchport mode trunkFixes the port as a trunk — tagged, multi-VLAN.
switchport trunk allowed vlan 10,20,30Restricts which VLANs are actually permitted across the trunk, out of every VLAN that exists on the switch.

Creating a VLAN and assigning membership

Two separate actions: creating the VLAN itself, then telling a specific port which VLAN it belongs to. Click a step to expand it.

enable
configure terminal
vlan 10
 name SALES
exit

This alone doesn't move any traffic yet — it only defines VLAN 10 to exist on this switch, with a human-readable name attached. Naming is optional but strongly recommended; an unnamed VLAN 10 six months from now is just a number.

interface fastEthernet0/1
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 10

switchport mode access fixes the port as a single-VLAN access port (as opposed to a trunk, Unit 3.2). switchport access vlan 10 is the line that actually moves this specific port's traffic into VLAN 10.

interface range fastEthernet0/2 - 9
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 10

interface range applies the same configuration to every interface in the range in one pass — the practical way to configure a block of ports for the same department or VLAN rather than repeating the same three lines nine times.

vlan 110
 name VOICE
exit
interface fastEthernet0/1
 switchport mode access
 switchport access vlan 10
 switchport voice vlan 110

A single access port can carry one data VLAN and one voice VLAN simultaneously — a common pattern for a PC connected through the back of an IP phone. The phone tags its own traffic into VLAN 110; the PC's traffic stays untagged in VLAN 10.

Static assignment is what this course covers What's shown above — an administrator manually binding a specific port to a specific VLAN — is called static (port-based) VLAN membership, and it's how the overwhelming majority of real networks are built. Dynamic membership (VMPS, assigning VLANs by MAC address rather than by port) exists but is legacy and rarely deployed today; 802.1X-based dynamic assignment through a RADIUS server is its modern replacement, and is out of scope for this unit.

Verifying VLAN configuration and membership

The same principle from Unit 2.4 applies here — an accepted switchport access vlan command doesn't confirm the port is actually in that VLAN and passing traffic correctly.

CommandWhat it confirms
show vlan briefEvery VLAN defined on the switch, its name, and which access ports belong to each — the fastest first check.
show vlan id 10Detail for a single VLAN — useful once the switch has many VLANs and the brief output is too long to scan.
show interfaces fastEthernet0/1 switchportA single port's administrative and operational mode (access/trunk), its access VLAN, and its voice VLAN if any — the direct way to confirm one specific port's membership.
show running-config interface fastEthernet0/1The exact configuration lines applied to that interface — confirms what was typed actually saved to the running config.
show mac address-table vlan 10Which MAC addresses the switch has learned specifically within VLAN 10 — ties back to Unit 2.3's MAC table, now scoped per VLAN.

Caution

The specific VLAN mistakes that look like a cabling or connectivity problem, but are actually configuration.

!

Assigning a port to a VLAN that doesn't exist yet leaves it inactive

If switchport access vlan 30 is configured before vlan 30 has ever been created, the port doesn't error out — it silently sits in an inactive state (visible in show interfaces status from Unit 2.4) until the VLAN is actually created. It looks like a link problem; it's a missing VLAN.

!

Deleting a VLAN doesn't move its ports back to VLAN 1

If VLAN 10 is deleted while ports are still assigned to it, those ports don't fall back to the default VLAN automatically — they go inactive, exactly like the case above, and stay that way until reassigned to a VLAN that exists.

!

VLAN 1, and VLANs 1002–1005, can't be deleted

Attempting no vlan 1 is rejected by IOS. Ports can be moved off VLAN 1 onto other VLANs, but VLAN 1 itself always continues to exist as the factory default.

!

A VLAN number is not an IP subnet

VLAN 10 and subnet 10.10.10.0/24 look related but nothing in IOS forces that mapping — it's purely a convention an administrator chooses and documents. Nothing stops VLAN 10 from being assigned any subnet at all, which is exactly why the documentation habit in Industry convention below matters.

!

erase startup-config still doesn't touch VLAN definitions

As covered in Unit 2.4: VLAN information lives in vlan.dat on flash, separate from startup-config. A switch "reset to factory defaults" that skipped delete vlan.dat will boot back up with all the same VLANs already defined.

Tips

Habits that keep a VLAN scheme readable months later, not just working today.

Always name the VLAN

An unnamed VLAN0010 in show vlan brief tells the next engineer nothing. A name does.

vlan 10 / name SALES

Keep ordinary devices off VLAN 1

Since VLAN 1 can't be deleted and carries switch control-plane traffic by default, many production networks deliberately leave it unused for end devices and assign a different VLAN for everyday user traffic.

Check before deleting

show vlan id 10 lists every port still assigned before removing a VLAN — cheaper than discovering the affected ports after they've gone inactive.

Filter long VLAN output

The same piping habit from Unit 2.4 applies here.

show vlan brief | include SALES

Industry convention

How VLAN numbering and documentation tend to look once a design has to scale past a handful of departments.

Numbering schemes

Small sites often number by department (10 = Sales, 20 = HR, 30 = IT); larger, multi-site designs commonly encode the site into the number too — e.g. VLAN 110 for Sales at Site 1, VLAN 210 for Sales at Site 2 — so a VLAN ID alone hints at both function and location.

VLAN ↔ subnet mapping

Production networks nearly always fix a 1:1 relationship between a VLAN and an IP subnet, and document it in an IPAM tool or spreadsheet — precisely because IOS itself doesn't enforce any such relationship (see Caution above).

Dedicated management VLAN

The switch's own management interface almost always sits in its own VLAN, reachable only from a restricted management network — not mixed in with user data VLANs or left on VLAN 1.

Voice VLANs as standard, not exception

Anywhere IP phones are deployed, a separate voice VLAN per access-layer switch is standard practice, both for QoS prioritization of voice traffic and to keep phone firmware/traffic logically separate from the attached PC.

Change control on VLAN deletion

Because deleting a VLAN silently orphans its ports (Caution above), production changes involving VLAN removal are typically preceded by an audit of every port and trunk still referencing that VLAN ID.

Recap

1A collision domain is bounded by a switch port and is largely a non-issue on modern full-duplex switching; a broadcast domain is bounded only by a router/Layer 3 interface, and a plain switch never breaks one up on its own.
2A Layer 2 loop forms from individually-correct switch behavior — each switch floods a broadcast out every other port, including back toward where it just came from — and, with no TTL field in Ethernet, the resulting storm never expires on its own.
3STP is enabled by default on Cisco switches specifically to block one of the redundant paths before a storm can start; the scenario at the top of this page describes what happens when that protection is absent, not its normal behavior.
4Without loop prevention, the failure is self-reinforcing: CPU spent flooding duplicated frames, bandwidth saturated by exponential frame growth, and a constantly-thrashing MAC table — together capable of taking a switch from idle to unresponsive in under a minute.
5A VLAN is a logical broadcast domain, independent of physical port location — it segments traffic without requiring separate physical switches, and it shrinks a storm's blast radius, though it doesn't by itself prevent a loop.
6Every port runs in a mode that decides its tagging behavior: access sends everything untagged for one VLAN, trunk tags everything except its native VLAN across several VLANs, and hybrid (not a Cisco term, but the same underlying idea) mixes tagged and untagged VLANs on one link — which is exactly what a Cisco access port with a voice VLAN already does.
7VLAN 1 is the unremovable default; data, voice, and management VLANs each carry a different kind of traffic, and best practice keeps them separate.
8Normal range is 1–1005 (1002–1005 reserved); extended range 1006–4094 needs care with older VTP versions.
9Creating a VLAN and assigning a port to it are two separate steps — vlan <id> then switchport access vlan <id> on the interface, and a port pointed at a VLAN that doesn't exist (or was deleted) goes inactive rather than erroring or falling back to VLAN 1.
10Verification is separate from configuration here too — show vlan brief and show interfaces switchport confirm what actually happened.
11A VLAN ID has no built-in relationship to an IP subnet — that mapping is an administrative convention, which is exactly why production networks document it explicitly.
