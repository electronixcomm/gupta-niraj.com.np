How the pieces fit together

This section moves from the general idea to one specific protocol to that protocol’s versions. Each branch below is one block of the lecture.

01

Distance and vector: what a router really knows

Every routing protocol has to answer one question: how does a router find out about networks it isn’t attached to? A distance-vector protocol answers by asking its neighbors. Each router tells its directly connected neighbors what it can reach and how far away it is; those neighbors add their own distance and pass it on.

Two facts travel with every route, and they give the family its name. The distance is the metric — hop count, in RIP. The vector is the direction: which neighbor to hand the packet to. That is all a router ever learns about a remote network.

FIG 1 · three routers in a line, and what each one actually knows

That is why distance-vector routing is nicknamed routing by rumor. Each router believes its neighbor’s summary and has no way to check it against the real topology — the root of both the protocol’s simplicity and its problems.

What every distance-vector protocol has in common

CharacteristicWhat it means in practice
Bellman-Ford algorithmAdd the link cost to the neighbor’s advertised distance; keep the smallest total
Periodic updatesThe whole routing table is sent on a timer, changed or not
Neighbors onlyUpdates go to directly connected routers, never flooded across the network
Simple metricOne number per route (hop count in RIP)
Slow convergenceNews of a change moves one hop per update cycle
Light on resourcesLittle CPU and memory, easy to configure
Loop-proneRouters can’t verify each other’s claims — the subject of 5.2

RIP (all three versions) and the obsolete IGRP are classic distance-vector protocols. EIGRP is often called advanced distance vector: it keeps the neighbor-based view but uses a much smarter update process, and belongs to Unit 6.

02

Using an update: add the link cost, then compare

The rule a router applies to every route it hears is short enough to memorize. Take the neighbor’s distance, add the cost of the link to that neighbor — in RIP, always 1 — and compare the total with what the routing table already holds.

R2 tells R1: "Net C is 1 hop from me." R1: 1 hop (R2's distance) + 1 hop (link R1–R2) = 2 hops R1: installs Net C, metric 2, next hop R2

Implementations differ on whether that extra hop is added by the sender or the receiver, but the result is identical. The comparison that follows has four cases:

SituationWhat the router does
Network not in the tableInstall it, with the sender as next hop
In the table, new metric is betterReplace the old route
In the table, same metric, different neighborKeep both as equal-cost paths — RIP load-balances across up to 4 by default
Update comes from the current next hopAlways accept it, even if the metric got worse — that neighbor knows its own path best

If the resulting metric reaches 16, the route is treated as unreachable. That rule is what keeps RIP from counting forever, and Section 5.2 shows exactly why it is needed.

03

RIP at a glance

RIP — the Routing Information Protocol — is the oldest distance-vector protocol still in use. It is simple and nearly universal, which is why it is the first dynamic protocol most students meet.

PropertyValueNote
MetricHop countOne per router crossed; ignores bandwidth and delay
Usable range1 – 1516 means unreachable
Administrative distance120Less trusted than static (1), EIGRP (90) and OSPF (110) — recall Unit 4, Fig 3
TransportUDP 520RIPng uses UDP 521
Update timer30 secondsFull table, every RIP interface
Load balancingEqual-cost only4 paths by default, 6 at most on Cisco

RIP speaks with just two message types. A Request asks a neighbor for its routes; a Response carries them. When a RIP router starts, it sends a Request out every RIP interface, then begins advertising its own routes. Ordinary periodic updates are Responses that nobody asked for.

04

Three versions, one family

RIPv1 (RFC 1058, 1988) is the original. RIPv2 (RFC 2453) keeps the same mechanism and adds the fields RIPv1 was missing. RIPng (RFC 2080) is the same protocol logic re-fitted for IPv6.

Multicast is the quiet improvement. A broadcast is processed by every host on the segment; a multicast to 224.0.0.9 is picked up only by routers running RIPv2, so hosts are no longer interrupted by routing chatter.

What RIPv2 actually added

RIPv1 sends each route as a 20-byte entry with three fields left at zero. RIPv2 uses that spare space, and the change explains almost every difference in the table above.

FIG 2 · a RIP route entry, RIPv1 vs. RIPv2 (teal = new in v2)

The subnet mask is what makes RIPv2 classless. The next hop lets a router point neighbors at a better gateway on a shared segment, and the route tag marks where a route originated (for example, redistributed from another protocol). RIPv2 authentication borrows the first entry slot, so an authenticated message carries 24 routes instead of 25.

COMMON MIX-UP · there is no RIPv3

The RIP family has exactly three members: RIPv1, RIPv2 and RIPng. If a colleague, a question paper or a tool says “RIPv3”, they almost certainly mean RIPng, the IPv6 version — or the name is a mistake. Write RIPng in an exam.

WHY THE MASK MATTERS · how RIPv1 guesses

RIPv1 updates carry no mask, so the receiver has to guess. If the advertised network is in the same classful network as the interface it arrived on, it applies that interface’s mask; otherwise it assumes the default class mask (/8, /16 or /24). That guess is right only when every subnet of a network uses one mask and all subnets sit together — which is why RIPv1 cannot handle VLSM or discontiguous networks.

RIPng in brief

  • It is the smallest change to RIP that works over IPv6: same hop-count metric, same 30-second timer, same infinity of 16.
  • Updates are sent from the router’s link-local address to FF02::9, so neighbors use that link-local address as the next hop.
  • Each entry holds a 128-bit prefix, a route tag, a prefix length and a metric. The entries per message depend on the interface MTU, not a fixed 25.
  • RIPng is a separate process from IPv4 RIP. A dual-stack router needs both configured.

The same idea in RIPng

R1(config)# ipv6 unicast-routing R1(config)# ipv6 router rip LAB R1(config)# interface g0/0 R1(config-if)# ipv6 rip LAB enable R1(config)# interface g0/1 R1(config-if)# ipv6 rip LAB enable

There is no network command in RIPng. It is switched on per interface, and LAB is just a locally significant process name. Check it with show ipv6 route rip and show ipv6 rip.

06

Terms to lock in before the next class

Distance vector

A routing approach in which each router knows only a distance and a next-hop direction for each network, learned from neighbors.

Hop count

RIP’s metric: the number of routers a packet crosses to reach a network. 16 means unreachable.

Classless (RIPv2)

Updates carry the subnet mask, so VLSM, CIDR and discontiguous networks work.

Passive interface

An interface that receives but does not send RIP updates, while its network is still advertised.

07

Check-in questions (last 10 minutes)

Q1

R2 advertises Net C to R1 as 3 hops away. What metric and next hop does R1 install, assuming it had no route to Net C before?

Reveal answer
Metric 4, next hop R2. R1 adds 1 for the link to R2 (3 + 1 = 4), and the neighbor that told it about the route becomes the next hop.
Q2

A network uses 172.16.1.0/24 and 172.16.2.0/26 on different links, and you want routing updates that only routers process. Which RIP version, and which two features justify it?

Reveal answer
RIPv2. It carries the subnet mask in every entry, so the mixed /24 and /26 subnets (VLSM) are understood, and it sends to multicast 224.0.0.9, so hosts don’t process the updates.
Q3

A colleague says, “We’re moving to RIPv3 so we can run RIP over IPv6.” What is wrong with that sentence?

Reveal answer
There is no RIPv3. The IPv6 version of RIP is RIPng (RFC 2080), which uses UDP 521 and multicast FF02::9 and is enabled per interface rather than with a network command.

RIP Timers, Updates & Loop Prevention

01

The update cycle and the four timers

A RIP router never asks “has anything changed?” It sends its whole table on a fixed schedule, and it judges its neighbors by whether they keep sending. Four timers run that schedule.

TimerDefaultRestarts whenAt expiry
Update30 sIt firesThe full routing table goes out every RIP interface
Invalid180 sAn update for that route arrivesThe route is marked invalid: metric 16, advertised as unreachable
Hold-down180 sStarts once a route is declared unreachableUntil it ends, worse information about the route is ignored
Flush240 sAn update for that route arrivesThe route is removed from the routing table
R1(config)# router rip R1(config-router)# timers basic 30 180 180 240 ^update ^invalid ^hold-down ^flush — keep identical on every router

One route’s life, from its last update

The timers make sense when you follow a single route after its neighbor stops advertising it. Every arriving update resets the clock; silence lets it run out.

FIG 1 · a route whose neighbor has gone quiet

While the route sits between 180 and 240 seconds it is still in the table, marked with metric 16, and the router keeps telling its neighbors it is unreachable. That is deliberate: it is how the bad news spreads.

Periodic and triggered updates

Periodic updates are the 30-second heartbeat. Triggered updates are sent immediately when a route changes — a link goes down, a metric changes — without waiting for the timer. They matter because waiting up to 30 seconds at every hop would make bad news travel painfully slowly. A triggered update carries only the routes that changed.

02

Why loops form: count-to-infinity

A distance-vector router cannot tell whether a neighbor’s route to a network passes back through itself. When a link fails, that blind spot lets two routers trade the same stale route back and forth, raising the metric each time.

Take two routers, with Net N attached to R1. R1 is 0 hops from N and R2 is 1. The link between R1 and N fails.

  1. R1 knows immediately and marks N unreachable.
  2. Before R1 can tell anyone, R2’s regular update arrives: “I can reach N in 1 hop.”
  3. R1 believes it and installs N at 2 hops via R2 — although R2’s route runs back through R1.
  4. R1 advertises 2, so R2 changes to 3 via R1. R2 advertises 3, so R1 changes to 4. And so on.

The metric climbs until it reaches 16, RIP’s definition of infinity. Only then is N declared unreachable. Until then packets for N bounce between the two routers until their TTL runs out, and reconvergence takes minutes.

03

Loop prevention: six mechanisms, six jobs

No single fix covers every case, so RIP stacks several. Each one closes a different gap in the failure sequence above.

Split horizon

A router does not advertise a route out of the interface it learned that route from. In the example, R2 learned N from R1, so it must never tell R1 about N — and the step-3 mistake cannot happen.

FIG 3 · what R2 may and may not advertise

All six mechanisms

MechanismWhat it doesWhat it prevents
Infinity = 16Puts an upper bound on the metricCounting forever (at the cost of the 15-hop limit)
Split horizonNever advertises a route out of the interface it was learned onSimple two-router loops. On by default on Cisco.
Route poisoningAdvertises a failed route with metric 16Neighbors continuing to trust a dead route
Poison reverseAdvertises a route back out its learned interface, at metric 16A neighbor mistakenly routing back through you
Hold-downFor 180 s after a route is unreachable, ignores worse information about itStale updates reinstating a dead route
Triggered updatesSends changes at once, not on the next 30 s tickBad news crawling hop by hop
COMMON MIX-UP · split horizon is not a complete cure

Split horizon stops the two-router loop in FIG 2. A loop that runs through three or more routers can still form, because no single router sees itself being advertised its own route. That is why poisoning, hold-down and triggered updates exist alongside it — each covers a case the others miss.

Seeing split horizon in a debug

Using the lab from 5.1: R1 learns LAN B (192.168.2.0/24) from R2 on g0/1. Watch what it leaves out when it sends back on that same interface.

(Cisco adds the hop when sending, so a connected network goes out with metric 1.)

To turn the behavior off on an interface for a lab experiment, use no ip split-horizon; ip split-horizon turns it back on. Do this on a lab router only.

04

Terms to lock in before the next class

Invalid timer

180 s without an update marks a route unreachable (metric 16). The route stays in the table, and is advertised as down, until the flush timer ends.

Hold-down

A period after a route is declared unreachable in which the router ignores worse information about it.

Split horizon

A route is never advertised out of the interface it was learned on.

Poison reverse

A route is advertised back out its learned interface, but with metric 16 so the neighbor won’t use it.

05

Check-in questions (last 10 minutes)

Q1

On default timers, R1 last heard about 192.168.2.0/24 exactly 200 seconds ago. What state is the route in?

Reveal answer
Invalid. The 180-second invalid timer has expired, so the route is marked with metric 16 and advertised as unreachable. It is not yet flushed, because the 240-second flush timer has not expired.
Q2

What is the difference between split horizon and poison reverse?

Reveal answer
Split horizon stays silent: the route is simply left out of updates sent on the interface it was learned from. Poison reverse speaks up: the route is included, but with metric 16, so the neighbor is explicitly told not to use it.
Q3

If split horizon is on, why are hold-down timers and triggered updates still needed?

Reveal answer
Split horizon only prevents loops between directly connected pairs. A loop through three or more routers can still form, and bad news still has to spread quickly. Hold-down blocks stale information from reinstating a dead route, and triggered updates spread the failure at once.

Limitations of RIP in Large Networks

Everything that makes RIP easy to run — a simple metric, a hard ceiling, a blunt update schedule — becomes a cost as the network grows.

Section 5.3
00

By the end of this hour

  • Explain why hop count can select a poor path, using bandwidth as the counter-example.
  • Explain the 15-hop ceiling and why it exists.
  • Estimate how slowly RIP detects and spreads a failure.
  • Calculate the periodic update load created by a large routing table.
  • Name RIP’s design and security limits, and say when RIP is still a sensible choice.
01

Hop count is not cost

RIP counts routers. It does not know whether a link is a gigabit fiber run or a 128 kbps serial line, how busy it is, or how long a packet takes to cross it. Every link is worth exactly one hop.

FIG 1 · two paths to the same network

Bandwidth, delay, load and reliability all go unseen. RIP will send bulk traffic down the serial line, congesting it, while the fast path sits idle. Protocols with richer metrics (OSPF uses link cost; EIGRP combines bandwidth and delay) avoid this — Unit 6.

02

The 15-hop ceiling

A metric of 16 means unreachable, so a RIP network can be at most 15 routers across. Any destination further away simply does not exist as far as RIP is concerned.

FIG 2 · every value a RIP metric can take
12345678910111213141516

15 is the last usable hop. 16 (coral) means unreachable.

The ceiling is not an arbitrary choice. It is the price of stopping count-to-infinity (5.2): the metric has to end somewhere, and it has to end soon enough that the counting finishes within minutes. A larger infinity would make loops last longer.

03

Slow to converge, heavy to run

RIP’s timers were chosen for simplicity, not speed. That shows up in two ways: how long a failure takes to notice and spread, and how much bandwidth the routine updates consume.

ProblemCauseConsequence
Slow failure detectionInvalid timer of 180 sIf a neighbor dies silently, a route can stay usable in the table for up to 3 minutes
Slow spread of newsOne hop per 30 s updateAcross a 15-hop chain without triggered updates: 15 × 30 s = 450 s, about 7.5 minutes
Loops during convergenceCount-to-infinityPackets bounce between routers until TTL expires

The cost of sending everything, every time

Because the entire table goes out every 30 seconds, update traffic grows in step with table size — whether or not anything changed. Each message holds at most 25 routes.

Those figures ignore IP and UDP headers, and every RIP interface pays them. On a modern LAN this is small; on a slow WAN link, most of it is repeating information the neighbor already has.

04

Design and security limits

  • Flat design. RIP has no areas or hierarchy, and its only summarization is at classful boundaries, so every router carries every route.
  • RIPv1 is classful. No VLSM, and it fails on discontiguous networks.
  • Weak security. RIPv1 has no authentication at all, so anyone on the segment can inject routes. RIPv2 offers plain-text or MD5 authentication, and RIPng leaves it entirely to IPsec.
  • Equal-cost balancing only. Unequal paths cannot be shared, so a spare, slower path sits idle.
COMMON MIX-UP · why auto-summary breaks discontiguous networks

Suppose 10.1.0.0/16 and 10.2.0.0/16 sit at opposite ends of a network, separated by a 172.16.0.0 link. With auto-summary on, each end advertises the classful summary 10.0.0.0/8 across the 172.16 link. The middle routers now see two equal routes to “10.0.0.0/8” from opposite directions, and traffic is split unpredictably. The fix is RIPv2 with no auto-summary.

RIPv2 MD5 authentication takes a key chain and one command per interface:

R1(config)# key chain RIPKEY R1(config-keychain)# key 1 R1(config-keychain-key)# key-string S3cretKey R1(config)# interface g0/1 R1(config-if)# ip rip authentication mode md5 R1(config-if)# ip rip authentication key-chain RIPKEY

Both ends of the link must use the same key, or the routers will silently ignore each other’s updates.

05

When RIP is still the right tool

These limits do not make RIP useless. They define where it is a sensible choice and where another protocol should take over.

ScenarioBetter fit
Small lab or classroom network, a handful of routersRIPv2 — quick to configure, easy to observe and debug
Small office, one path between sites, no redundancyRIPv2 — the simplicity is worth more than the missing features
Older equipment that only speaks RIPRIP — near-universal support
More than 15 hops between any two pointsOSPF or EIGRP — RIP cannot reach that far
Mixed link speeds where path quality mattersOSPF or EIGRP — metrics that reflect bandwidth
Large, changing network needing fast failoverOSPF or EIGRP — faster convergence, less update traffic

Hold onto that last row for Unit 6: link-state protocols exist precisely to give every router the full map, so they converge faster and do not depend on rumor.

06

Terms to lock in

Convergence

The state in which every router in the network has a consistent, correct routing table.

Auto-summary

Summarizing routes to their classful boundary when advertising across a different major network.

Discontiguous network

Subnets of one major network separated by a different major network.

Infinity (16)

07

Check-in questions (last 10 minutes)

Q1

RIP prefers a 1-hop 128 kbps link over a 3-hop path made of gigabit links. Which limitation is this, and what kind of protocol avoids it?

Reveal answer
RIP’s hop-count metric, which ignores bandwidth and delay. A protocol whose metric reflects link speed — OSPF (cost) or EIGRP (bandwidth and delay) — would choose the fast path.
Q2

A campus has 20 routers in a straight line. Can RIP carry routes from one end to the other? Why or why not?

Reveal answer
No. The far end is 19 hops away, past the 15-hop limit. A metric of 16 means unreachable, so routers beyond hop 15 are never learned.
Q3

A router holds 500 RIP routes. How many messages does it send on each RIP interface every 30 seconds, and why is that wasteful?

Reveal answer
20 messages (500 ÷ 25). It is wasteful because the whole table is sent every time, even when nothing has changed, so on a slow link most of the traffic repeats information the neighbor already has.
